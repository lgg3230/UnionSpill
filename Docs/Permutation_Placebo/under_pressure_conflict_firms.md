# Under Pressure: Global Evidence on Conflict and Firms

## Authors and Publication

**Emanuele Brancati**, **Michele Di Maio**, **Roberta Gatti**, **Asif M. Islam**

Journal of Development Economics, Volume 182, 2026, 103807

## Abstract

This paper provides the first global analysis of the impact of conflict exposure on firm performance, combining geolocalized longitudinal firm-level data with information on political violence events across 89 countries between 2006 and 2019. Our results show that higher conflict exposure leads to declines in both sales and total costs, resulting in no significant effect on profits for surviving firms. The reduction in sales is driven by lower output, which reflects conflict-induced shortages of raw materials and production inputs, as well as increased informal competition. Firms respond to declining sales by adjusting labor costs, substituting skilled workers with unskilled ones. The negative effects of conflict on firms are more pronounced in countries that are more involved in international trade, have weaker institutions, infrastructure, and financial development, were initially at peace, or are characterized by more spatially diffuse conflict. In these settings, greater conflict exposure leads to reductions in both sales and profits.

**Keywords:** Political violence, Conflict, Firm performance, Intermediate inputs, Labor market, Market competition, Informality

**JEL Classification:** H56, D22, E26, O17

## 1. Introduction

Conflict and political violence have become the new normal in many developing and advanced economies, with over 1.7 billion people worldwide living in fragile or conflict-affected countries. In 2024 alone, political violence incidents were reported in 167 nations, with 50 experiencing extreme, high, or turbulent levels of conflict—resulting in more than 233,000 fatalities (ACLED, 2024). 

Despite the severe suffering and disruption caused by political violence and conflict, economic activity continues. Economists and policymakers are increasingly interested in understanding how firms adapt under such challenging conditions. A growing number of studies provide rigorous estimates of the effects of conflict on firm performance and offer in-depth analyses of the underlying mechanisms (see, e.g., Amodio and Di Maio, 2018; Couttenier et al., 2022; Korovkin and Makarin, 2023; Bernal et al., 2024). However, research on firm behavior in settings characterized by political violence and conflict remains limited to a small number of countries, and existing findings are largely country- and conflict-specific.

This paper is the first study to provide a global perspective on the impact of conflict on a range of firm-level outcomes and the mechanisms underlying these effects. Moreover, by exploiting differences in cross-country characteristics, it shows how economic, institutional, and political factors shape the way in which firms cope with conflict exposure.

Our analysis relies on the combination of two main data sources. First, we use a version of the global World Bank Enterprise Survey (WBES), which, beyond the rich firm-level data in the public version, includes geolocation information for each firm. We focus on its panel component, covering 89 countries from 2006 to 2019. Second, we draw on the Integrated Crisis Early Warning System (ICEWS) dataset to obtain geolocated data on conflict and political violence events in each country. This allows us to construct a firm-specific, time-varying measure of conflict exposure, which we use in a firm-level fixed-effects model to estimate the impact of conflict on several outcomes.

This study yields a number of empirical findings. We show that higher conflict exposure—as measured by the number of political violence events occurring in the neighborhood around a firm—leads to a decline in both sales and spending on production inputs, indicating a contraction in output. These adjustments substantially limit the impact on profits for surviving firms, resulting in smaller and imprecisely estimated effects on average profitability. However, the effect of conflict exposure on profits is negative and statistically significant for firms in countries with specific characteristics.

We address several potential identification threats to the interpretation of our empirical results. First, we tackle the issue of reverse causality by showing that the local intensity of conflict is not driven by the level or structure of local economic activity, nor is the occurrence or intensity of conflict events systematically related to firm characteristics. We also show that our results remain robust across several alternative specifications. These include controlling for placebo firm exposure to account for potential non-random assignment of conflict events, restricting the estimation sample to clean controls, and incorporating the conflict exposure of suppliers and buyers or neighboring firms to address potential violations of the SUTVA. Moreover, we address the possibility that our findings are driven by selection on unobservables. Finally, we show that our conclusions are robust to a range of sample restrictions, alternative definitions of conflict exposure—including variations in buffer size, time window, event type, and data source—as well as to different choices for clustering standard errors.

Next, we explore the mechanisms through which conflict exposure reduces sales and total costs, ultimately leading to a decline in output. We first show that conflict leads to a reduction in firms' purchases of intermediate inputs and raw materials—both domestic and imported—as well as electricity. Given that prices are unlikely to decrease during periods of conflict, this offers indirect evidence that firms are purchasing fewer production inputs. Furthermore, conflict disrupts access to these inputs by increasing import clearance times and intensifying both the frequency and duration of power outages, further contributing to the decline in output. In addition, conflict heightens competitive pressure from informal firms. Since such competition reduces output, employment, and investment within the formal sector, this channel further helps to explain the observed decline in sales.

Faced with these pressures, firms adjust their cost structures in an attempt to cushion adverse effects on profits. While limited access to inputs mechanically lowers expenditures, firms also reduce labor costs by substituting skilled workers with unskilled ones. We also find some suggestive evidence that conflict may reduce sales by decreasing both domestic and foreign demand. In contrast, other potential channels—such as increased credit constraints, corruption, or crime—do not appear to explain our findings. Taken together, these results indicate that conflict reduces sales through lower output, intensified informal competition, and diminished demand, while profits exhibit less pronounced variation due to firms' adaptive cost-cutting responses.

A key advantage of using a harmonized global dataset is that it allows us to compare the effects of conflict on firms across countries that differ in their economic and institutional characteristics. There is no heterogeneity in the impact of conflict exposure on firms located in countries that differ by per-capita income, level of state fragility, or reliance on agriculture and natural resources. By contrast, substantial variation emerges along other dimensions. We find that conflict exposure has a more negative effect on sales for firms operating in economies with lower human capital and weaker labor market. Moreover, the negative effects of conflict are amplified, implying also a reduction in profits, for firms located in countries more exposed to international trade or less complex, with low financial and infrastructure development, lower bureaucratic quality and higher corruption, and characterized by higher criminal activity.

Importantly, the effects also differ depending on a country's initial conflict status and the spatial distribution of political violent events. Higher conflict exposure leads to declines in sales and, in some cases, profits for firms in countries that were initially at peace or where political violence events are more spatially diffuse. Moreover, we show that the mechanisms through which conflict exposure operates on firm performance vary systematically across countries. This highlights the importance of accounting for country-specific characteristics when assessing the impact of conflict on firms, as the underlying mechanisms—and the appropriate policy responses—can vary substantially across contexts.

Our paper contributes to the growing literature on the microeconomic consequences of conflict. This strand of research has largely focused on outcomes related to education and health (for reviews, see Verwimp et al., 2019; Rohner, 2023). In contrast, studies examining the economic effects of conflict remain relatively limited—partly due to the difficulty of accessing reliable economic data in conflict-affected settings. While early work explored the macroeconomic effects of conflict (e.g., Abadie and Gardeazabal, 2003; Martin et al., 2008; Glick and Taylor, 2010), more recent empirical studies have shifted attention to its impact on firms. These studies examine various forms of conflict and political violence in specific country contexts—including Afghanistan (Blumenstock et al., 2024; Neri-Lainé, 2025), Angola (Guidolin and La Ferrara, 2007), Colombia (Camacho and Rodriguez, 2013; De Roux and Martinez, 2023; Bernal et al., 2024), India (Couttenier et al., 2022), Kenya (Ksoll et al., 2023), Libya (Del Prete et al., 2023), Mozambique (Custodio et al., 2025), Ukraine (Korovkin et al., 2024; Korovkin and Makarin, 2023), Sierra Leone (Collier and Duponchel, 2013), and the West Bank (Amodio and Di Maio, 2018). However, these are all single-country analyses that focus on different outcomes, rely on distinct data sources, and employ different methodological approaches, making their results not directly comparable.

This paper contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it provides the first global analysis of the impact of conflict on firms, offering a more comprehensive perspective than previous single-country studies. Leveraging harmonized firm-level data from both developed and developing countries, we document several empirical regularities that hold across different economic and institutional contexts. Second, we document several mechanisms through which conflict adversely affects firms. Our results confirm that a key mechanism driving the negative impact on firm activity is the conflict-induced reduction in access to both domestic and imported production inputs, consistent with findings from previous studies. In addition, we provide evidence on two novel mechanisms. Conflict increases competitive pressure from informal firms—an important and well-established obstacle to formal firms' output and sales. We also find evidence of a conflict-induced substitution effect in the workforce, leading to a shift toward lower-skilled labor. Third, we provide novel insights into how country-level characteristics shape the impact of conflict on firms. We show that economic, structural, social, and political conditions can either amplify or attenuate the effects of conflict exposure. Furthermore, we show that the specific mechanisms through which conflict affects firm activity vary systematically with these characteristics, suggesting that policy interventions to address the effects of conflict need to be context-specific and tailored to the conditions in which firms operate.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the data. Section 3 outlines the empirical strategy. Section 4 presents the results. Section 5 concludes.

## 2. Data

### 2.1. Firm data

Our main data source is the World Bank Enterprise Survey (WBES), a global dataset providing firm-level information for the manufacturing, retail, and other service sectors across 148 countries. Data are collected through face-to-face interviews using a standardized questionnaire and are designed to be representative at the country level. The survey targets privately owned firms with at least five employees operating in the formal (non-agricultural) sector. Our data cover the years 2006–2019 and exclude the COVID-19 pandemic period.

We use a version of the WBES global dataset, which also provides firms' geo-localization. This information allows us to match each firm with conflict and political violence events occurring in its geographical neighborhood and is therefore essential for constructing the firm-specific measure of conflict exposure used in our analysis. Another important feature of the WBES is that—although designed as a repeated cross-section—it includes a sizable panel component for a subset of countries, with some firms interviewed in multiple waves. As discussed in Section 3, our estimation strategy relies on firms for which geo-localized data are available and that belong to this panel component. Restricting further to firms with non-missing sales data, our main estimating sample consists of 36,087 firm-year observations across 89 countries. Table A1 provides a detailed list of the countries and survey waves included in our analysis, while Fig. 1 shows their geographical distribution.

As noted by Besley and Mueller (2018), there is a trade-off in using the WBES. While in some countries the information is less detailed than that found in national-level manufacturing surveys, the WBES offers several clear advantages. It provides harmonized information across a large and diverse set of countries, covers multiple sectors, and includes data on specific obstacles and market characteristics—such as informal competition—that are typically unavailable in other surveys. Moreover, given the focus of our analysis and the empirical strategy employed, which requires longitudinal firm-level information and geolocalization, the WBES offers better coverage than any other global dataset, including ORBIS Bureau van Dijk.

A typical firm in our sample is domestically owned and operates in the private sector. As shown in Table A2, the median firm has 22 employees and has been operating for 16 years. It mainly serves the domestic market—only 16% of firms are exporters—and generates 334,700 USD in annual sales and 271,800 USD in annual profits (in 2000 constant USD). All variables are defined in Table C1.

#### Selection, attrition, and missing values

As discussed in Section 3, our estimating strategy relies on the subset of firms with multiple observations over time, i.e., those included in the panel component of the WBES. This may raise concerns about potential selection issues affecting our estimating sample. In particular, one might worry about the self-selection of firms that are re-interviewed in subsequent survey waves, vis-à-vis firms that, for various reasons, are interviewed only once. If such selection is correlated with our main regressors of interest and outcome variables, it could bias our results. In Table A3, we test whether firms included in the panel (and thus in our estimation sample) differ systematically from those in the cross-section. Specifically, we consider all firms in countries with at least two survey waves and for which geolocalization data are available. We then examine whether the likelihood of being interviewed a second time—i.e., of being included in the panel—is correlated with key variables used in the analysis: Sales, Total costs, Profits, and Conflict exposure (defined below in Section 2.2). Results in columns 1–5 show that the probability of inclusion in the estimation sample is not driven by these characteristics, allaying concerns about systematic differences between panel and non-panel firms.

Another potential concern with our data is that missing values may be correlated with conflict exposure, possibly biasing the estimation sample. Results reported in Table A4 suggest that this is not the case. Column 1 shows that, in the full sample, our measure of conflict exposure is not correlated with firms not reporting the sales. Column 2 confirms that this result also holds in the subsample of firms for which baseline control variables are available. Moreover, in the estimation sample, conflict exposure is not correlated with non-reporting of raw material and intermediate input costs (column 3) or labor costs (column 4). Taken together, these results indicate that, for the firm outcome variables collected in the WBES, non-reporting is not systematically associated with conflict exposure.

Finally, we explore potential issues related to attrition. In Table A5, we test whether conflict exposure is correlated with firms exiting our sample. As expected, the results show that firms are more likely to exit when they are exposed to a higher number of conflict events between two survey waves and when their pre-exit sales levels are lower. This pattern suggests that, if anything, our estimates of the negative effect of conflict on sales may be downward biased—potentially underestimating the broader impact of conflict on economic activity.

### 2.2. Conflict data

Our primary source of data on political violence events is the Integrated Crisis Early Warning System (ICEWS; Shilliday and Lautenschlager, 2012). For each event, ICEWS provides information on the date, the actors involved, and the precise location (geospatial coordinates). The dataset records a wide range of interactions—both cooperative and hostile—among sociopolitical actors, including individuals, groups, and nation-states. Events are classified using the Conflict and Mediation Event Observations (CAMEO) framework (Schrodt and Yilmaz, 2012), with each category assigned an intensity score ranging from −10 to +10, ranking events from the most hostile to the most cooperative.

We construct our panel dataset of events by selecting all hostile entries in the ICEWS dataset—i.e., those with negative intensity values—that are classified as violent according to Amodio et al. (2021). This includes a broad spectrum of political violence and conflict episodes, even those that did not result in fatalities. Our final dataset comprises a total of 1.1 million political violence events occurring between 2006 and 2019. A detailed breakdown of event types and their frequency over the period of analysis is provided in Table A6, while Fig. 2 displays their geographical distribution.

We exploit these data to construct our firm-specific measure of exposure to political violence, labeled Conflict Exposure. This variable is defined as the logarithm (log of 1+) of the number of political violence events that occurred within a 20 km radius of the firm's location during the 12 months preceding the end of the last fiscal year. In absolute terms, the mean is 14, the standard deviation is 8.2, and the median and third quartile are 15 and 83, respectively.

As robustness checks, we construct alternative measures of conflict exposure: (i) by using different radii for the buffer around each firm or weighting by inverse distance; (ii) by exploring different types of conflict events; and (iii) by employing alternative data from the PRIO/Uppsala Armed Conflict and Location Event Dataset (ACLED) (Raleigh et al., 2010). Like the ICEWS dataset, ACLED provides the date and geolocation of several types of conflict-related events. However, ACLED's country coverage during our period of analysis is significantly smaller.

Our measures of conflict exposure may be subject to measurement error, as the ICEWS dataset shares common limitations of large-scale datasets compiled from multiple sources. One such issue is the presence of duplicated events, which we address by excluding entries with identical characteristics (e.g., date, location, actors). Another potential source of measurement error is related to possible biases in event reporting, which may favor certain locations. To mitigate this concern, we account for potential (time-invariant) geographical differences in reporting by including firm and area fixed effects in our analysis. Additionally, we cross-validate ICEWS conflict event data with information from the ACLED dataset. The correlation between the number of conflict events reported by the two datasets is very high (0.77) within the same sample. Robustness checks further confirm that our main results hold when using ACLED as a dataset for conflict events.

### 2.3. Additional data

In the heterogeneity analysis, we draw on a wide range of additional data sources to classify countries across several economic, institutional, and political dimensions. Detailed definitions and data sources are provided in Table C1.

**Income:** Countries are grouped by their level of economic development, following the World Bank classification.

**Fragility:** We use the World Bank categorization to identify fragile countries—those experiencing deteriorating governance, prolonged political crises, post-conflict transitions, or gradual but still unstable reform processes. In our sample, 16% of countries fall into this category.

**Trade, agriculture, and natural resources:** Countries are classified based on indicators from the World Bank capturing key features of their economic structure: (i) merchandise exports and imports as a share of GDP, capturing engagement in international trade; (ii) value added in agriculture, reflecting the importance of agricultural activity; and (iii) the share of natural resources in GDP.

**Economic complexity:** Countries are categorized according to the complexity of their production structure using the Economic Complexity Index (ECI, Hidalgo and Hausmann, 2009). The ECI has been shown to explain income differences across countries and to predict future growth (see, e.g., Hausmann et al., 2014).

**Human capital and labor market characteristics:** We capture country-level human capital and labor-market conditions using World Bank indicators on (i) the duration of compulsory education (in years), (ii) the unemployment rate, (iii) the self-employment rate, and (iv) the international migrant stock as a share of the population.

**Quality of infrastructure and financial development:** To proxy the quality of domestic infrastructure and financial systems, we use World Bank indicators on access to electricity (as a measure of basic infrastructure quality) and domestic credit to the private sector (as a proxy for financial development).

**Bureaucracy quality:** The quality of a country's bureaucracy is measured using data from the International Country Risk Guide (ICRG, Political Risk Services Group). This index captures the institutional strength and quality of the civil service, including its ability to function without political interference, ensure continuity in government policy, and effectively implement reforms.

**Corruption:** Country-level corruption is measured using an index from the ICRG that captures perceptions of corruption within the political system. This includes the misuse of public office for private gain through practices such as bribery, patronage, and nepotism.

**Production of illegal drugs:** Major illicit drug-producing and/or drug-transit countries are identified based on the International Narcotics Control Strategy Report, published by the U.S. Bureau for International Narcotics and Law Enforcement Affairs.

**Peace/conflict status and spatial distribution of conflict events:** Countries are grouped based on their peace or conflict status at the beginning of the analysis period. A country is classified as initially at peace if, at the time of the first survey in that country, there are no major episodes of political violence (such as civil conflicts, ethnic violence, riots, popular protests, or repression of dissidents). Countries not meeting this criterion are classified as initially in conflict. To capture the spatial distribution of conflict events, we construct a normalized Herfindahl–Hirschman concentration index based on conflict events aggregated to a 20 km-by-20 km grid at the country level. Lower values indicate more spatially dispersed conflict. See Table C1 for details.

## 3. Empirical strategy

We estimate the impact of conflict exposure on firms' economic performance by combining longitudinal firm-level data with geolocalized information on conflict events. Our main specification reads as follows:

Y_it = α + β Conflict exposure_it + γ' X_it + μ_i + θ_t + ε_it                    (1)

where Y_it represents the outcome of interest for firm i in fiscal year t. The analysis considers several firm-level outcomes as dependent variables, including Sales, Total Costs, and Profits. Conflict exposure denotes the firm-specific measure of conflict exposure. In our baseline specification, it is computed as the logarithm (log of 1+) of the number of conflict events that occurred within a 20 km radius of the firm's location during the 12 months preceding the end of the last fiscal year. The vector X_it represents a set of time-varying firm characteristics, including size, age, and export status. μ_i denotes firm fixed effects, accounting for all time-invariant observed and unobserved characteristics of the firm that could potentially influence the outcome of interest. θ_t represents year fixed effects, controlling for overall trends in economic activity and common shocks.

As a robustness check, we also include the full set of area-year interacted fixed effects to account for all time-varying location-specific determinants of firm performance. Finally, ε_it is the error term. In all specifications, we report robust standard errors clustered at the firm level, which is the unit of observation used to measure the intensity of conflict exposure. All variables are defined in Table C1.

Our identification strategy relies on the assumption that, conditional on our full set of fixed effects, conflict events occurring near the firm's location are uncorrelated with any latent determinant of its economic performance. Under this assumption, β captures the reduced-form effect of firm-specific conflict exposure on economic performance. In what follows, we present our core results and examine in detail potential threats to the validity of our identification strategy.

## 4. Results

### 4.1. Conflict exposure, sales, total costs, and profits

Table 1 presents our main results. Column 1 shows that conflict exposure leads to a large and statistically significant decline in firm sales. This effect remains robust in column 2 after the inclusion of firm-level controls. Based on our estimates, a 50% increase in the number of conflict events occurring within a 20 km radius of the firm during the last fiscal year reduces median sales by about 8%, corresponding to approximately 28,500 USD.

Higher conflict exposure also leads to a significant decline in total production costs, measured by expenditures on raw materials, intermediate inputs, and labor (column 3). Results do not change when including controls (column 4).

These reductions in both sales and total costs could reflect changes in either prices or quantities. Although the WBES dataset does not allow us to directly test for price changes, it is unlikely that conflict lowers consumer or input prices. This suggests that the observed declines in sales and total production costs are mainly driven by a contraction in output and a reduction in the quantity of inputs and materials used in production.

Finally, we find no statistically significant effect of conflict exposure on firms' profits (columns 5 and 6). This null result should be interpreted with appropriate caution for two main reasons. First, while the estimated coefficient is smaller than those for sales and costs, it is not negligible in magnitude and always negative. Second, the lack of precision in the estimate may partly reflect the fact that profits are a constructed outcome, combining self-reported sales and costs, and must be transformed using the inverse hyperbolic sine to accommodate zero and negative values—both of which increase the variance of the dependent variable. In any case, either a null or a marginally negative effect on profits is consistent with our result that conflict exposure affects both sales and total costs in the same direction, suggesting that firms respond to reduced sales by reducing input purchases and adjusting labor composition. Because these adjustments imply a contraction in output, conflict-related violence ultimately translates into lower aggregate economic activity, even when the profitability of surviving firms appears relatively little affected on average.

Our estimates can be used to assess the aggregate effects of conflict exposure on growth dynamics. As our main outcome is log sales, the coefficient in Table 1 captures differences between more- and less-exposed firms. A negative estimate therefore implies slower sales growth among exposed firms, without necessarily indicating an absolute decline: whether a firm contracts in levels or simply grows more slowly depends on its pre-conflict trajectory. To map firm-level elasticities into aggregate effects in a tractable way, we adopt a back-of-the-envelope approach providing a simple accounting framework to quantify the aggregate economic costs of conflict exposure. To do so, we rely on the WBES's retrospective reporting of sales two fiscal years prior to the interview, which provides an observed sales level immediately preceding the period in which we begin measuring conflict exposure. Using this observation as a common benchmark, we construct a simple counterfactual that removes the estimated exposure effect in the fiscal year for which sales are observed, recompute firm-level growth from this benchmark, and aggregate the resulting firm-level outcomes to the country–year level. This provides a transparent mapping from the micro-level estimates to implied aggregate growth losses.

Table 2 reports these results. Averaged across countries, realized annual sales growth is about 6%, compared with 23% under the no-conflict counterfactual, implying a mean gap of 17 percentage points (pp). The aggregate measure yields a very similar estimate, corresponding to a loss of about 21 pp. The large magnitude of these gaps reflects the choice of the benchmark counterfactual. Setting exposure to zero provides a direct and transparent mapping from the estimated firm-level elasticity to aggregate outcomes, but it represents an extreme scenario and therefore delivers an upper bound on the gains from reduced violence. Under more conservative counterfactuals—reducing exposure to the 25th or 50th percentiles of the country–year distribution, rather than setting it to zero—the implied gains are substantially smaller, averaging around 6 and 3 pp, respectively. These comparisons indicate that while the benchmark captures the full economic cost associated with conflict exposure, more limited reductions in violence would translate into smaller, though still economically meaningful, improvements in aggregate sales growth.

Country-level patterns are heterogeneous in magnitude but display a consistent pattern in outcomes. In some countries, firms experience outright contractions relative to the pre-period benchmark—most notably in Afghanistan (−17%), Zimbabwe (−14%), Bolivia (−8%), and Kenya (−3%), among others. In these cases, the no-conflict counterfactual implies positive growth, indicating sizable losses associated with conflict exposure. In other cases, firms continue to grow, but at rates well below those implied by the no-conflict counterfactual. For example, in DRC realized sales growth is about 4%, compared with 28% in the counterfactual, while in Nigeria growth is 6% vs 22%. The largest gaps arise in Bangladesh (34 pp), West Bank and Gaza (33 pp), Lebanon (30 pp), and Egypt (29 pp), where realized growth is close to zero or negative while counterfactual growth is strongly positive. When we consider more realistic counterfactual scenarios—reducing exposure to the 25th or 50th percentiles—the implied gains at the country level are correspondingly smaller in magnitude, though still economically sizable.

#### Threats to identification

The firm-level fixed effects in our baseline specification account for all time-invariant firm, sector, and geographical characteristics. However, our estimates may still capture factors that vary over time at the local level and are correlated with both the number of conflict events and firms' economic performance. To address this concern, we augment model (1) with a full set of area–time interacted fixed effects, thereby controlling for all time-varying, location-specific determinants of firm performance, such as infrastructure availability and local labor market conditions. Table A7, columns 1–2, shows that the coefficient for Sales remains negative, sizable, and statistically significant, although smaller than in the baseline, while the coefficient for Profits remains statistically insignificant. Importantly, including area–time fixed effects changes the source of identifying variation: estimates are based solely on within-area comparisons across firms exposed to different levels of conflict. As a result, the estimated coefficient excludes the component of the effect operating through area-wide changes in economic conditions. This specification therefore identifies a within-area effect of conflict exposure, whereas the baseline also captures its broader impact through area-wide effects. Both specifications are informative and capture different margins of adjustment; reassuringly, the results are qualitatively consistent across them. We retain the baseline as our preferred specification to capture the full economic effect of conflict, which is particularly relevant for the aggregate implications discussed above.

Another potential threat to our identification is reverse causality. To address this, we start by including the lead number of conflict events in our baseline regression for both Sales and Profits (see Table A7, columns 3–4, respectively). Reassuringly, for both outcomes, the results are consistent with the baseline, and the coefficients for the lead variables are centered around zero. This pattern indicates that future changes in conflict are not driven by current firm performance, which helps rule out reverse causality and reassures about parallel trends in firm outcomes.

A related concern is that the negative relationship between sales and conflict might reflect the targeting of low-performing firms. However, this does not appear to be the case. As shown in Table A26 (columns 5–7), conflict exposure does not increase firms' losses from theft.

We also consider the possibility that poor local economic conditions may themselves trigger violent events. To address this concern, we conduct two additional tests. Table A8 shows that local conflict intensity—defined at the second-level administrative unit (i.e., GID2)—is not predicted by the level of economic activity. Specifically, we find no correlation between conflict intensity in a given area and various proxies for economic activity measured in the preceding period—namely, total sales, total employment, total profits, sales growth, and employment growth (columns 1–5, respectively). This result holds whether conflict intensity is measured in the following year, over the next two years, or over the next three years (reported in panels A, B, and C, respectively).

At the same time, results in Table A9 rule out the possibility that conflict intensity is driven by the local composition of economic activity. There is no significant correlation between the economic size of any sector and subsequent conflict intensity in the same area—whether measured one, two, or three years later (columns 1–3).

Another concern is that the location and intensity of conflict events may instead be driven by the presence of firms with better economic performance, making them valuable targets for conflict fighters. As an additional indirect test for reverse causality, we re-estimate our model by excluding large firms, those with high sales and profits, and those located in capital cities. The results, reported in Table A10, are virtually identical to those obtained using the full sample. This indicates that our findings are not driven by the location and intensity of conflict events being influenced by the presence of high-value firms or by the activity of firms located in political centers.

Finally, we address the concern that firms may be nonrandomly exposed to conflict events by implementing a test in the spirit of Borusyak and Hull (2023). We construct two sets of placebo controls designed to account for the possibility that firms are exposed to conflict in a systematic, predictable way. In the first exercise, we hold the firm's location fixed but randomize the timing of conflict events by assigning each firm its actual conflict exposure from previous years (t−2 to t−5), drawn from a uniform distribution. This placebo measure is then included as an additional control in the baseline sales regression to capture any predictable component of conflict exposure driven by past patterns. In the second exercise, we construct a placebo measure based on the number of conflict events affecting other firms operating in the same year and geographical area. In both cases, we repeat the randomization process 5000 times and estimate the marginal effect of actual conflict exposure, controlling for the placebo measure. Figure A1 shows that the distribution of our estimates remains centered around our main coefficient, suggesting that the results are not driven by nonrandom assignment of conflict exposure.

Conflict exposure is heterogeneous across firms and varies over time. In a two-way fixed-effects model, this type of treatment may lead to the negative weighting problem (De Chaisemartin and d'Haultfoeuille, 2020; Goodman-Bacon, 2021). To check whether this is an issue in our case, we perform a clean control analysis through sample restriction in the spirit of De Chaisemartin et al. (2022). Table A11 presents the results. As a preliminary step for this analysis, in column 1, we replicate Table 1 using a dummy variable as an alternative measure of conflict. The indicator I(Conflict exposure ≥ p(25)) equals one if the firm is exposed to a number of conflict events above the 25th percentile of the country–year distribution, and zero otherwise. The results indicate that the estimated effect is negative and highly significant. Once we confirm that our results continue to hold using a dummy treatment variable, in columns 2 and 3 we refine the analysis using a "clean" sample of movers and stayers. In the spirit of the methodology proposed by De Chaisemartin et al. (2022), we classify movers as treated units and quasi-stayers as controls. In this framework, we define movers as firms with substantial treatment intensity—those at or above the 25th percentile of the treatment distribution—and quasi-stayers as firms with negligible exposure, falling below that threshold. In each survey wave, we exclude firms that had already been significantly exposed to conflict. This allows us to compare outcomes between firms experiencing conflict for the first time and those that remain untreated or have not yet been treated. To further refine the sample, we exclude firms with significant conflict exposure in the two years prior to the survey wave. Results in Table A11, columns 2 and 3, show that our findings remain robust across all specifications in this clean controls analysis, using the same dummy treatment variable as in column 1: conflict exposure reduces sales and has no significant effect on profits.

Overall, this analysis suggests that the observed effects are not driven by heterogeneous treatment responses within our empirical framework.

To support our interpretation of the main results, we also conduct a formal check for selection on unobservables using the test proposed by Altonji et al. (2005) and further refined by Oster (2019). Table A12 presents the estimated impact of conflict exposure under varying assumptions about the relative importance of unobservables, captured by the parameter 0 < δ ≤ 1. Column 1 reports the baseline effects of conflict exposure on sales and total costs (corresponding to columns 4 and 6 in Table 1, respectively). Columns 2–11 show that the estimated coefficient on conflict exposure remains negative across all values of δ ∈ (0,1). Column 12 reports the value of δ required to drive the estimated effect to zero. In both cases, δ is negative and large in absolute value, implying that selection on unobservables would need to be strong and of the opposite sign to selection on observables for the effect to be nullified by the bias. These results suggest that our main findings are unlikely to be driven by selection bias from unobserved factors.

One final potential concern with our results is a possible violation of the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA), which requires that a firm's response depends solely on its own level of conflict exposure, and not on the exposure of other firms. In our setting, the estimated effect of conflict exposure on firm i could be biased if firms in other areas—linked to firm i through supplier or buyer relationships—are also affected by conflict events. In such cases, conflict would exert both a direct effect—through firm i's own exposure—and an indirect effect—through network linkages. To address this concern, we check whether our results change when accounting for supplier and buyer network spillovers in the regression. Ideally, this analysis would require firm-to-firm transaction data for each country. However, such data are not available for the vast majority of countries in our sample. As an alternative, we rely on OECD input–output tables by country and use the WBES identification of the main product sold (ISIC 4-digits) to construct a product–country-specific matrix of input–output relationships. For each firm in our sample, we construct two weighting matrices: one reflecting dependence on input purchases and another on output sales. By interacting these matrices with the level of conflict exposure experienced by firms in the supplier and buyer networks, we test whether a firm—beyond being directly affected by conflict—is also indirectly impacted through its production linkages.

The top panel of Table 3 replicates our main results while accounting for potential spillovers of conflict transmitted through the domestic production network. Across all outcomes, the indirect effect of conflict exposure through these linkages is not statistically significant, while the direct effect remains consistent with our baseline findings—negative and significant for sales and total costs, and insignificant for profits. Notably, the negative, though imprecise, spillover effect on sales is consistent with previous studies on conflict and production networks (Couttenier et al., 2022; Korovkin and Makarin, 2023; Korovkin et al., 2024).

Although estimated with considerable noise, the spillover coefficients are economically sizable and align with the patterns documented in the literature. The lack of statistical precision in these estimates points to limitations in measuring network-based exposure, rather than to the absence of spillover effects per se. Accordingly, we cannot rule out economically meaningful input–output spillovers. Importantly, the estimated coefficient on direct conflict exposure remains highly stable across all specifications, indicating that any such indirect effects do not bias our main results.

After accounting for the potential propagation of conflict through production linkages, we also examine whether similar effects arise through geographic proximity. While input–output spillovers capture indirect transmission along supply chains, spatial spillovers reflect the diffusion of conflict impacts among firms operating within the same local economy. The bottom panel of Table 3 investigates this possibility by testing whether firms are indirectly affected through spatial proximity to other exposed firms. Specifically, we define Neighbor conflict exposure as the inverse-distance–weighted average of nearby firms' exposure to conflict events within 20 km of their own location, considering neighbors located within a 100 km radius. The estimated coefficient on this variable is virtually zero and statistically insignificant across all specifications, indicating that conflict does not appear to propagate through spatial proximity beyond the direct exposure already captured by the 20 km buffer around each firm. This finding is reassuring for two reasons. First, it indicates that the baseline estimates are unlikely to be driven by unaccounted local spillovers among nearby firms, which could otherwise threaten the SUTVA. Second, it supports the interpretation that our measure of direct exposure captures the relevant spatial scale at which conflict affects firm outcomes. Together, these results support the validity of our identification strategy and the robustness of the estimated effects to potential spatial dependence.

#### Robustness

Our results are robust to a number of checks. First, we examine the estimation sample. As discussed in Section 2.1, the number of observations varies across outcomes due to missing values. All results presented thus far are based on the maximum number of available observations for each outcome in each regression. Table A13 shows that results are unchanged when we restrict the analysis to a balanced sample, i.e., firms with data available for all the main outcomes. As an additional check, we re-run the main analysis focusing only on manufacturing firms. This ensures that our results are not driven by differences in the construction of Total costs across sectors (see Section 2.1). Table A14 shows that our results continue to hold also in this case.

Next, we assess the robustness of our findings to the countries included in the analysis. We re-run our baseline regression 89 times, sequentially excluding one country at a time from the sample. Figure A2 shows that the negative effects on sales and total costs, and the insignificant effect on profits, do not depend on the inclusion or exclusion of any specific country.

Our conclusions are also robust to alternative definitions of conflict exposure. Table A15 shows that the estimated effects of conflict on our main outcomes remain qualitatively unchanged when exposure is defined using: (i) smaller buffers around the firm (5 km and 10 km radii in columns 1–2); (ii) a larger buffer (40 km radius, column 3); (iii) a weighted measure based on the inverse distance of conflict events (column 4); and (iv) conflict events occurring within a 20 km radius over the previous two years (column 5).

Table A16 reports the results obtained when we separate conflict exposure into mutually exclusive distance bands around each firm. This specification allows us to assess how the magnitude of the estimated effects varies with the proximity of conflict events. The coefficients show a clear attenuation pattern: the impact is largest for conflicts occurring within 20 km of the firm and declines steadily for more distant events. This monotonic decay with distance provides direct evidence that spatial proximity is central to the mechanism through which conflict affects firm performance. The strong and significant coefficient for the 0–20 km band confirms that the baseline results are driven by events occurring in close proximity to firms, while exposure to conflicts beyond this range has little influence.

Additionally, we examine how our findings depend on the types of events included in our measure of conflict exposure, namely all hostile events in the ICEWS dataset that are defined as violent according to Amodio et al. (2021) (see Table A6). Table A17, column 1, shows the results when we exclude from this list those events that may be less likely to have a direct economic impact on the firm. Column 2 reports estimates obtained by excluding events related to protests and riots. Columns 3 and 4 apply a quantitative event-selection rule based on the intensity score (see Section 2.2): column 3 focuses on highly violent events, defined as those with intensity scores between −8 and −10, while column 4 restricts attention to the most severe events, corresponding to an intensity score of −10, such as killings or suicide attacks. In all cases, our baseline results are confirmed. Finally, column 5 shows that the results for both sales and total costs remain consistent when using the ACLED dataset to measure conflict exposure, with the effect on profits being negative and marginally significant. These findings are reassuring, given that ACLED covers a substantially different—and smaller—set of countries, as well as a distinct set of conflict-related events compared to ICEWS. While all our results are consistent across these alternative definitions of conflict exposure, they also suggest that firms respond differently depending on the type of event, with the negative effects of conflict being more pronounced when the events are more violent.

Finally, Table A18 confirms that our inference is robust to alternative assumptions about spatial correlation in the residuals. We re-estimate the baseline specification using four clustering schemes that allow for different forms of local dependence—within area–year cells, within 0.5° latitude–longitude grid–year cells (approximately 50 km), and using Conley-type standard errors (Colella et al., 2023) that permit correlation to decay smoothly over space within 20 or 100 km. Across all specifications, the estimated standard errors are very similar, and the conflict coefficient remains highly significant, indicating that the precision of our estimates is not affected by spatially correlated shocks.

### 4.2. Mechanisms

Our results show that conflict exposure reduces a firm's sales and total costs. These effects are likely driven by a combination of production-side and market-level channels. One mechanism relates to the availability of production inputs. Higher conflict exposure may constrain access to domestic and foreign inputs—such as raw materials, intermediate goods, electricity, or labor—forcing firms to adopt a sub-optimal input mix. This reduction in input quantity and quality lowers overall output and, in turn, depresses sales. At the same time, conflict may reduce sales by changing the level and type of competition faced by firms. In particular, it may increase competition from informal firms, a phenomenon well known to have negative consequences for formal ones. Finally, conflict may lower sales by decreasing demand or increasing the severity of the obstacles to a firm's activity. In what follows, we take these hypotheses to the data to examine the mechanisms driving the negative effect of conflict on firms' sales and production input expenditures.

#### 4.2.1. Raw materials and intermediate inputs

Table 4, column 1, shows that conflict exposure leads to a decline in firms' expenditures on raw materials and intermediate inputs. This reduction could reflect either lower input prices or a decrease in the quantity of inputs purchased. As the WBES does not report prices and quantities separately, we cannot test this directly. However, given that conflict is unlikely to lower the unit cost of inputs, we interpret these findings as evidence of a conflict-induced reduction in the quantity of raw materials and intermediate inputs used in production. Consistent with this interpretation, conflict also appears to reduce electricity use, another key production input. Firms more exposed to conflict events report lower expenditures on electricity (column 2) and a higher likelihood of experiencing power outages (column 3), suggesting that conflict constrains the availability of electricity and forces firms to reduce its usage. Furthermore, conflict decreases the share of imported intermediate inputs used in production (column 4) and, by the same argument, likely reduces their quantities as well. Importantly, the results in Table A19 suggest that the decline is driven by reduced availability of imported inputs, rather than by a decrease in firms' demand. Conflict appears to increase the difficulty of importing, thereby limiting access to foreign production inputs. Column 1 shows that, among firms using imported inputs, those more exposed to conflict are more likely to report customs procedures as a significant obstacle to their operations. Column 2 further indicates that higher conflict exposure is associated with a longer time required to clear customs for imported goods. These findings align with previous single-country studies indicating that conflict hampers firms' access to imported inputs due to increased trade costs or uncertainty. Given that imported inputs are crucial for firm productivity, a negative shock to their availability likely leads to a significant reduction in firms' economic activity. Taken together, these findings suggest that conflict reduces the availability of both raw materials and intermediate inputs, especially imported ones, resulting in a decline in firms' overall output and, consequently, sales.

#### 4.2.2. Labor

Table 4, column 5, shows that conflict exposure leads to a decline in total labor costs. To shed light on the mechanisms behind this effect, columns 6–9 explore changes in employment levels, workforce composition, the allocation of workers to protection activities, and average wages. Conflict exposure does not appear to affect the total number of workers employed by the average firm (column 6), where employment includes both full-time permanent employees and temporary workers expressed in full-time equivalents. However, it does alter the composition of the workforce, resulting in a sizable increase in the share of unskilled workers (column 7). Column 8 shows that conflict exposure is also associated with a mildly significant increase in the share of workers devoted to protection activities. This measure, constructed following Besley and Mueller (2018), captures the proportion of employees engaged in non-productive, security-related tasks, serving as a proxy for the misallocation of labor from production to protection. This pattern suggests that firms exposed to conflict divert part of their workforce toward safeguarding assets and personnel. Unreported regressions show that this is primarily driven by smaller firms, while the effect is negligible for large ones, which are plausibly already better equipped to protect assets and personnel. Finally, consistent with these adjustments, higher conflict exposure also leads to a reduction in the average wage (column 9), which helps explain the overall decline in total labor costs.

Table A22 provides suggestive evidence supporting the idea that firms reduce total labor costs by substituting skilled labor with less expensive unskilled workers. As shown in column 1, the substitution effect is more pronounced among firms producing non-differentiated products, which are typically more reliant on cost efficiency and less dependent on specialized labor. This is expected, as producing differentiated goods relies more heavily on higher-skilled labor, limiting firms' ability to substitute toward lower-skilled workers. Additionally, column 2 indicates that the magnitude of the substitution effect varies with a country's labor legislation. In contexts where firing costs are higher (proxied by severance pay levels), the substitution of skilled workers with unskilled ones in response to conflict exposure is smaller. These results align with the idea that worker substitution is a defensive strategy employed by firms to reduce labor costs when affected by conflict: when sales decrease due to lower output from reduced availability of production inputs, firms adjust their workforce to lower costs.

The remaining columns of Table A22 help rule out alternative explanations for the observed reduction in total labor costs. Column 3 shows that the decline is not driven by an increased reliance on temporary workers, suggesting that changes in contract structure are not a key mechanism. Column 4 further indicates that the substitution away from skilled labor is not due to supply-side constraints—such as a conflict-induced decline in the availability of skilled workers. Specifically, firms do not report a worsening of the skilled labor supply as a more serious obstacle to their operations. Overall, these results provide evidence that the substitution of skilled with unskilled workers is primarily driven by demand-side adjustments—namely, firms responding to conflict-induced reductions in their production possibilities.

#### 4.2.3. Market competition

An important determinant of firm sales is the level of market competition. In Table 4, columns 10–11, we examine how conflict affects this dimension. Results indicate that conflict increases the competition faced by formal firms from informal ones. Conflict exposure increases the likelihood that firms report informal competition as an obstacle (column 10), and the perceived intensity of the threat posed by informal competitors (column 11).

The conflict-induced increase in informal competition is consistent with two distinct phenomena: the entry of new informal firms or the transition of existing formal firms into informality. Because the WBES samples only formally registered firms, we cannot directly distinguish between these channels. Nevertheless, the patterns in our data appear more consistent with the latter interpretation.

First, as shown in Table A23 (columns 1 and 3), conflict exposure does not seem to affect the (self-reported) total number of competitors. Second, conflict is associated with higher rates of firm exit from the WBES sample (Table A5). Importantly for our interpretation, these two effects are consistent, as exit from the survey does not necessarily imply firm closure. For instance, firms may disappear from the sample if they respond to conflict exposure by switching to informal activity or scale down production, ceasing to meet the survey's size eligibility criteria. These margins are likely to be particularly relevant for smaller firms, which tend to operate closer to the boundary between the formal and informal sector. To assess whether this channel is empirically relevant, Table A24 examines if conflict disproportionately affects firm exit at the lower end of the size distribution. The WBES includes only formally registered firms with at least five employees. We test whether firms just above this cutoff are more likely to exit the sample. We interact conflict exposure with indicators for firms near the minimum size requirement, using alternative thresholds of six and seven employees. We find that conflict exposure raises the probability of firm exit overall, with somewhat larger coefficients for firms near these thresholds. Although the differences are not statistically significant, this pattern is consistent with the interpretation that part of the observed exit reflects transitions into informality rather than market exit per se.

These results are consistent with prior evidence showing that informality tends to expand in response to adverse shocks, as firms seek to exploit the cost advantages of non-compliance with formal regulations (Ulyssea, 2018). A similar pattern emerges in conflict-affected settings: for instance, Neri-Lainé (2025) show that rising insecurity in Afghanistan reduces the presence of formal firms while simultaneously increasing informality. The resulting expansion of informal activity is not without consequences. A growing literature documents that higher informal competition reduces the output of formal firms (Rozo and Winkler, 2021), lowers their employment (Amin, 2023), weakens innovation incentives (Avenyo et al., 2021), and curbs credit demand and investment (Brancati et al., 2024). More broadly, the spread of informality contributes to resource misallocation and lower total factor productivity, often with significant welfare losses (Ulyssea, 2018). Therefore, conflict-induced increases in informal competition likely constitute a key transmission channel through which conflict weakens firm performance and reduces aggregate productivity, consistent with the decline in firms' sales documented in Table 1.

#### 4.2.4. Demand

An alternative explanation for the observed declines in sales and total costs is that conflict depresses consumer demand. In this scenario, firms would respond to reduced household expenditure by scaling back production and, in turn, decreasing input purchases, resulting in lower sales. Although we cannot directly test this channel due to the absence of consumer demand measures in the WBES, Table A25 provides suggestive evidence that demand-side effects alone are unlikely to fully explain for our findings.

Column 1 examines whether the effect of conflict exposure on sales varies with the firm's primary destination market—local, national, or international. If the observed decline in sales were driven solely by a conflict-induced contraction in domestic demand, firms serving international markets would be expected to be less affected. We find no evidence in support of this pattern. The estimated effect of conflict exposure on sales does not differ significantly across the three market categories. This indicates that the negative impact on sales is not confined to firms oriented toward domestic customers, making it unlikely that reduced consumer demand alone accounts for our results.

Next, we explicitly examine heterogeneity based on firms' export status. Results in column 2 show that the negative impact of conflict on sales is smaller for exporting firms, and column 3 indicates that this mitigating effect increases with the export share in total sales. However, even for firms that export 100% of their output, the effect remains economically and statistically significant. This implies that even firms with no domestic customer base are substantially affected by conflict occurring close to their location.

Finally, we examine the effect of conflict on capacity utilization to further assess whether demand-side mechanisms can account for our findings. The WBES defines Capacity utilization as a firm's actual output expressed as a percentage of its maximum potential output using all available inputs, providing a useful proxy for identifying demand-driven fluctuations in production. Because a firm's production capacity is closely tied to input availability, a decline in capacity utilization would indicate that firms are scaling back production in response to weaker demand. However, the estimates reported in Table A25, column 4, show that capacity utilization is not significantly affected by conflict exposure. This implies that the observed decline in sales is unlikely to be driven by underutilization of installed production capacity, as would be expected if firms were primarily responding to a conflict-induced contraction in demand.

Overall, these results indicate that although demand-side factors may contribute to the observed patterns, they are unlikely to be the primary driver of the decline in firm performance.

#### 4.2.5. Other obstacles to firms' activity

Finally, we investigate whether the decline in firms' sales can be attributed to a conflict-induced deterioration in business conditions. Results reported in Table A26 offer little support for this explanation. Columns 1–4 show estimates for several potential constraints, including access to finance, corruption, transportation, and land availability. Firms more exposed to conflict are no more likely to report these factors as severe obstacles, suggesting that they do not, on their own, play a substantial role in explaining the observed decline in performance. Similarly, column 5 indicates that the reduction in sales is not primarily driven by conflict-induced increases in exposure to crime, as measured by losses due to theft. Moreover, columns 6 and 7 show that conflict exposure does not increase security-related expenditure, as the corresponding coefficients are not significant and small. This implies that although firms may adjust security inputs in response to conflict (see Section 4.2.2), such adjustments are limited and unlikely to account for the observed decline in sales.

Overall, these findings indicate that a deterioration in business environment constraints does not appear to be the main channel through which conflict affects firm outcomes.

#### Summing up

Taken as a whole, our results indicate that conflict constrains firms' access to key intermediate inputs—particularly imported goods and electricity—leading to lower output. At the same time, conflict intensifies competitive pressure from the informal sector. Firms respond by adjusting their cost structure, including substituting skilled labor with unskilled workers. Consequently, sales decline due to both reduced output and heightened informal competition, while profits remain relatively more stable, reflecting firms' cost-cutting adjustments.

### 4.3. Heterogeneity

In this section, we examine the heterogeneous effects of conflict exposure on our main outcomes across several firm-level and country-level characteristics.

#### 4.3.1. Firm-level characteristics

Table A27 presents heterogeneity analyses based on several firm characteristics, including size, age, government ownership, and foreign ownership. The estimated effects of conflict exposure on sales and profits do not differ significantly across most dimensions. An exception is firm size: the negative impact on sales is significantly stronger among smaller firms. Beyond size, we find no systematic heterogeneity by age, government ownership, or foreign ownership. These patterns suggest that larger firms exhibit greater resilience to conflict—potentially reflecting superior access to resources or greater operational flexibility—while other firm-level attributes play a limited role in shaping the effects of conflict.

We also examine whether the effect of conflict exposure varies across sectors. Table A28 shows that the impact on sales is negative across all industries, with the exception of Other services and Non metallic, where the effect is statistically insignificant. Conflict exposure significantly reduces profits in only two sectors—Furniture and Transportation—where the negative effect is also the largest among all industries.

#### 4.3.2. Country-level characteristics

To examine how the effects of conflict exposure vary across settings, we analyze heterogeneity along a set of country-level characteristics capturing key dimensions that may shape the firms' capacity to cope with conflict-related disruptions. Specifically, we consider indicators of economic development, sectoral composition, and trade integration; measures of human capital and labor market conditions; proxies for infrastructure quality and financial development; as well as indicators of bureaucratic quality and the presence of organized crime. We additionally account for countries' prior exposure to conflict and the spatial concentration of political violence events, as both historical experience and geographic patterns may shape firms' responses. This framework allows us to assess whether the magnitude of conflict's effects is systematically related to structural features of the economy and the broader institutional settings in which firms operate.

Fig. 3 presents the heterogeneity results based on country-level characteristics measured at the beginning of the first survey wave in each country. The figure reports the estimated coefficients and 95% confidence intervals for the interaction terms between conflict exposure and each characteristic, shown separately for sales and profits.

We begin by examining characteristics that do not appear to shape the effect of conflict exposure on firms. In particular, being located in a high-income country does not, in itself, mitigate the negative impact of conflict on firm performance. Likewise, firms operating in fragile countries are not disproportionately more affected by conflict events. This finding suggests that, although fragility indicators are often used to assess conflict risk, they do not strongly predict the severity of its economic impact on firms within a given setting. Finally, the effects of conflict do not differ systematically across countries with greater reliance on agriculture or natural resource rents, indicating that the mechanisms documented in Section 4.2 operate largely independently of a country's sectoral specialization.

For other country characteristics, the magnitude of the estimated effects varies more markedly. In particular, dimensions related to labor-market conditions—such as human capital (proxied by the duration of compulsory education), self-employment, unemployment, and the international migrant stock—are associated with larger declines in sales following conflict exposure. Along these dimensions, there is no evidence of differential profit responses across groups.

Fig. 3 also highlights several structural and institutional characteristics for which conflict exposure is associated with more pronounced declines in both sales and profits. Specifically, firms operating in countries with greater exposure to international trade, lower economic complexity, weaker infrastructure quality, and more limited financial development tend to experience more severe effects. The larger effects observed in more trade-exposed economies are consistent with firms' stronger reliance on international markets on both the supply and demand sides. As discussed in Sections 4.2.1 and 4.2.4, conflict disrupts access to imported inputs and reduces export activity, making firms in trade-open environments especially vulnerable to breakdowns in global supply chains and foreign demand fluctuations.

Conflict exposure is also more detrimental in countries with lower economic complexity. Since economic complexity reflects the number and strength of domestic production linkages, this pattern highlights the difficulties faced by firms in less complex economies when substituting disrupted inputs or reorganizing supply chains in response to conflict, resulting in sharper declines in output and performance.

Beyond these structural characteristics, firms' capacity to adjust to conflict-related disruptions further shapes their vulnerability. Larger adverse effects are observed in countries with weaker infrastructure quality and more limited financial development, where poor-quality electricity supply or tighter credit constraints reduce firms' ability to buffer input shortages or reorganize production processes.

Institutional quality also plays a central role. The adverse effects of conflict exposure are amplified in countries characterized by low bureaucratic quality, higher levels of corruption, and the presence of organized criminal activity, as proxied by illicit drug production. In such settings, where allocation of resources is more likely mediated by corruption or criminal networks, disruptions to access to essential inputs and services—such as imported goods and electricity—translate into sharper declines in firm performance.

Taken together, these results indicate that the economic consequences of conflict for firms depend critically on country-level economic and institutional conditions. Firms operating in economies that are more exposed to international markets, have weaker infrastructures and financial structures, and function within a weaker institutional setting have access to fewer buffers against conflict-related disruptions.

We next assess whether the effects of conflict vary with a country's initial conflict status and the spatial distribution of violent events. We begin by testing whether firms in countries that were initially at peace respond differently to an increase in political violence than firms in countries already marked by an ongoing conflict (see Table C1 for definitions).

The sign of this heterogeneity is a priori ambiguous. On the one hand, firms in persistently violent environments may have developed coping strategies that reduce the marginal negative impact of additional conflict exposure. On the other hand, prolonged exposure to violence may exacerbate existing vulnerabilities, thereby amplifying the economic costs of conflict events. Fig. 3 shows that the economic consequences of conflict exposure are substantially larger for firms in countries that were initially at peace. In these settings, increased conflict exposure leads to a significantly larger decline in sales, and the impact on profits is also sizable. In countries initially in conflict, firms are more likely to have previously adapted their operations to such conditions. By contrast, firms in countries initially at peace appear less prepared to absorb conflict-related disruptions and therefore experience larger adverse effects. These results are largely unchanged when we adopt alternative definitions of countries' prior conflict status—whether based on the five years preceding the first survey, the full survey window, or the combination of both (see columns 1–3 of Table A30).

These findings suggest that cross-country heterogeneity in the effects of conflict exposure reflects differences in firms' capacity to adjust to variation in political violence intensity.

A second dimension concerns how political violence is distributed across space. For instance, localized regional insurrections may affect economic activity differently from conflicts dispersed across the entire country. We capture this using a concentration index based on the Herfindahl–Hirschman approach, adapted to the spatial distribution of conflict events on a 20 km-by-20 km grid at the country level. This measure allows us to differentiate countries where conflict is geographically concentrated from those where it is more spatially diffuse. Our results show that conflict is more detrimental in settings where events are widely dispersed, possibly indicating that diffuse conflict heightens firms' effective exposure through broader economic linkages even when the overall number of events is similar.

Taken together, these findings show that both a country's initial conflict status and the spatial dispersion of political violence events shape how severely firms are affected. Conflict events occurring in previously peaceful countries, or conflicts characterized by more spatially diffuse violence, impose greater adjustment costs and leave firms substantially more vulnerable to conflict-related disruptions.

#### Mechanisms heterogeneity: Country's conflict status and type of conflict

Country characteristics not only shape the effect of conflict on firm activity but may also influence the channels through which these effects operate. We therefore examine how the relative importance of key mechanisms varies with a country's initial conflict status and with the spatial diffusion of political violence. Table 5 summarizes the results.

Focusing on initial country conditions, columns 1–4 show that conflict exposure reduces spending on intermediate inputs and electricity and increases the likelihood of power outages in both types of countries. However, with the exception of the imported-input share, these effects are consistently more severe in countries initially at peace. Columns 5–9 reveal similar cross-country differences in labor-market adjustments. In countries initially at peace, firms respond to exposure by substituting skilled workers with unskilled labor, lowering average wages and total labor costs. By contrast, in countries already experiencing conflict, exposure primarily leads to reductions in total employment. This pattern is consistent with firms in conflict-affected countries having already adjusted their production structure, leaving reductions in employment as the primary remaining margin of adjustment. For the share of workers assigned to protection tasks, the estimated coefficient is somewhat larger in countries initially at peace, although the effects are imprecisely estimated. A similar pattern appears for informal competition. Higher conflict exposure does not materially affect competitive pressure from informal firms in countries already in conflict, whereas in countries initially at peace it increases both the likelihood of facing informal competitors and the perceived threat associated with such competition (columns 10–11). These cross-country differences align with our interpretation of the informal-competition mechanism as a conflict-induced adjustment process. In countries with pre-existing conflict, shifts toward informality may have already taken place, at least in part. In initially peaceful countries, new increases in conflict exposure trigger more pronounced adjustments along this margin. These results are largely unchanged when we adopt alternative definitions of countries' prior conflict exposure (columns 7–10 of Table A30).

Turning to the spatial distribution of political violence events, we find meaningful differences between countries with dispersed events and those where they are more geographically concentrated. In settings with widespread conflict, the reductions in spending on raw materials and electricity are stronger, the share of imported inputs falls more sharply, and labor-market adjustments—such as increases in unskilled employment and declines in unit wages—are more pronounced. Conflict events also has a stronger effect on informal competition in these countries, raising both the likelihood that firms face informal rivals and the perceived intensity of this competition. By contrast, two mechanisms respond more strongly when conflict is geographically concentrated: the probability of power outages and the share of workers assigned to protection tasks. These differences suggest that concentrated conflict strengthens localized disruptions, whereas dispersed conflict is associated with more extensive adjustments along input, labor, and competition channels. The differences in underlying mechanisms discussed above are consistent with the sharper declines in sales and profits observed among firms in countries initially at peace and in those with more spatially dispersed conflict, reinforcing the idea that the effects of conflict exposure—and the channels through which they operate—vary significantly with broader country characteristics.

## 5. Conclusions

Our analysis, covering 89 developed and developing countries from 2006 to 2019, offers a unique perspective on the effects of conflict and political violence on firm activity. By leveraging harmonized firm-level data, we document several empirical regularities that hold across different settings and contexts. The global scope of our analysis enables cross-country comparisons, providing novel insights and a more comprehensive understanding of how conflict affects firm performance across the world.

Our results show that higher exposure to conflict reduces both firm sales and production costs. Conflict disrupts access to domestic and imported intermediate inputs and raw materials, forcing firms to scale down production, which in turn lowers sales. Sales are further depressed by intensified competition from informal firms. While reduced output drives down production costs through lower input purchases, firms also respond to conflict by cutting labor costs—substituting skilled workers with unskilled ones. As a result of these adjustments in both sales and costs, the average effect of conflict exposure on profits is somewhat reduced. However, the estimated effect on profits is consistently negative and statistically significant in countries with specific characteristics.

A key advantage of our global analysis is that it allows us to provide novel evidence on how country-level characteristics shape the effects of conflict exposure on firm behavior. We find that conflict exposure has more severe effects for firms operating in countries that are initially at peace and where political violence events are more spatially dispersed, as well as in economies that are more exposed to international markets, less economically complex, and characterized by lower quality institutions, poorer infrastructure, and weaker financial institutions, and lager presence of criminal activity. In these contexts, conflict leads to substantial declines in sales and profits. Finally, we show that the relative importance of the mechanisms through which conflict affects firm performance also varies systematically with country characteristics.

Three policy implications emerge from our analysis. First, the quality of government institutions plays a critical role in helping firms cope with the adverse effects of conflict. Our findings highlight the importance of investing in state capacity and bureaucratic capabilities, particularly in settings characterized by high conflict risk. Second, economies with low complexity and high trade openness—typical of many developing countries reliant on a few primary sectors—are more severely affected by conflict. Policies that promote economic diversification, local input sourcing, and infrastructure development can enhance resilience to external shocks, including political violence. Third, the economic costs of conflict are disproportionately high for firms in countries that are initially at peace. This implies that, although early interventions to preserve peace—such as targeted diplomacy, inclusive political institutions, or conflict-sensitive development assistance—may be costly and complex, they can yield substantial returns. Our results suggest that both domestic policymakers and the international community should invest in conflict prevention, as the economic value of maintaining peace is significant, both within and across countries.
